# Narrator registers

The base voice is `docs/prose-style.md`: James S. A. Corey for the crew, Ursula K. Le Guin for the narrator, with the dialogue rules in `dialogue.md` and the passages in `reference.md`. A register is a tuning of that base, and the dial is choosing one per scene or per beat. The same moment is retold in each so the dial can be seen: the quarrel in the galley from reference passage 3.

The sentence-length figures are starting values. Check them against the samples and adjust the register, not the sample.

## plain

Sentence length: 6 to 40 words, mean about 17.
Build: Coordination and subordination in a mix. Openings other than subject and verb are welcome ("In the galley...", "When...", "Because..."). Fragments only in speech. Parallel structure at most once in a passage.
Rhythm: Opens on the place or the act, lengthens where a custom or a sequence of actions is carried, and ends on something said or done that is a little off the point.
Humor: Only what a character says.
Custom: The narrator states what is done aboard and why, once per scene, in a sentence of its own, and never what anyone feels about it. Ann Leckie is the model for stating a custom flatly and in full; take the custom-first statement.
North star: Patrick O'Brian. Take: the long balanced sentence that carries shop talk, and the way talk wanders about the work. Leave: the period diction.

Sample:

> "If you had said something," Ines says, "I would have moved it. I am not going to stand here and be told I did it on purpose, when I did not know it was a problem until Pax mentioned it at the watch change."
>
> "I said something." Mara puts the spoon down on the counter, bowl up. "Tuesday, at the watch change, in front of Pax, and you said you would look at it when you had a minute, and it has been six days, and the minute has not come."
>
> "Then I forgot."
>
> "You forgot." Mara looks around the table. "Did anyone hear me say it?"
>
> Nobody answers.

## dry

Sentence length: 8 to 35 words, mean about 16.
Build: Balanced sentences with a turn at the end. A subordinate clause sets up a plain fact, and one short sentence delivers the fact that is the joke. No fragments in narration.
Rhythm: Opens on the rule or the state of things, and ends on the plain fact, never on an explanation of it.
Humor: In what the characters say and in the situation itself, never in the narrator's comment. The narrator reports the absurd thing and stops.
Custom: Stated as the ship's own rule, with the humor in the rule and not in the telling.
North star: Lois McMaster Bujold. Take: the dry line a character says, and the balanced sentence with a turn. Leave: the wry narrator commentary and the heavy interior thought, which break rule 8 of `docs/prose-style.md`.

Sample:

> The galley has one thermostat, set into the bulkhead beside the coffee maker, and by long agreement it is moved only by whoever is prepared to be blamed for it. Tonight that is Ines, and Mara has come to say so. "If you had said something," Ines says, "I would have moved it back." "I said something." Mara puts the spoon on the counter, bowl up. "Tuesday, at the watch change, in front of Pax." Pax, three seats down with a tray of rice, goes on eating the rice. The display reads eighteen degrees. "Then I forgot," Ines says, and Mara asks the table whether anyone heard her say it, and the table passes the salt.

## tense

Sentence length: 4 to 30 words, mean about 12; no run of more than three short sentences.
Build: A short sentence for the change (the contact, the alarm), then one long sentence that carries the sequence of actions or the order. Participles and subordinate clauses for things happening at once. Commands in speech stay whole sentences.
Rhythm: Opens on the change, lengthens as people act, and ends on the next thing that has to be done, not a reflection.
Humor: None in narration. Speech may be dark, never a joke aimed at the reader.
Custom: Only the custom that matters now (who goes to which station, what is sealed first), with no background.
North star: None supplemental; the primary model is Corey, as in reference passage 5.

Sample:

> Mara is standing with the spoon in her fist and Ines has her back to the bulkhead, and the only way out of the galley is the strip of deck between them. "Say it again," Mara says. "I said I forgot," Ines says, "I did not do it to you, I forgot," and the second time she says it she is looking at the door. Pax comes in for water, sees the two of them, and stops with the cup half filled. At the end of the corridor the hatch seal cycles. The watch is coming off, and in a minute there will be four more people in the galley than there are now.

## quiet

Sentence length: 10 to 45 words, mean about 20.
Build: Long, fully built sentences with subordinate clauses, formal and even. No fragments. The thing that matters most is left out of the sentence and carried by an object or an act.
Rhythm: Opens on a plain fact of the ship, lengthens through the ordinary details, and ends in the middle of a routine, on an act and not an image.
Humor: Almost none; at most a character's own dry remark.
Custom: Stated in full, as the way the ship does something, with the feeling carried by the thing done. Ann Leckie is the model; take the custom-first statement.
North star: Kazuo Ishiguro. Take: the restraint, and the formal long syntax. Leave: the first-person unreliable narrator, since ours reports.

Sample:

> It is understood aboard that a quarrel in the galley is finished when the person who began it picks up a tray, and that nobody comments on whether the tray is full. Ines says, "If you had spoken, I would have moved the thermostat," and Mara, who has put her spoon down on the counter with the bowl turned up, says, "I did speak. Tuesday, at the watch change, with Pax standing there, and you said you would look at it when you had a minute." "Then I forgot," Ines says. Mara asks the table, "Did anyone hear me say it?" Nobody answers, and Ines takes a tray from the rack and fills it with what is left of the rice.
