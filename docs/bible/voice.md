# The voice

Status: approved by the owner on 2026-10-31, written from the samples the owner corrected in conversation. It replaced the old writing guides (`docs/prose-style.md`, `docs/prose-aims.md`, `docs/writing-a-scene.md` and `docs/voices/`, retired in #869). The world it uses is the rest of this bible.

The game's writing comes from the world. People talk about their lives, and their lives are the arrangements in this bible: rent, deductions, licences, charters, lag, old kit. The writer's job is to know those arrangements well enough that people can mention them without explaining them.

## The reference passage

> The seal on the coffee maker's boiler has gone soft. Tomas has the thing in pieces on the galley table and is printing a new seal on the shop fabricator, out of the stock they keep for the water lines. It isn't rated for heat. He'll log it as temporary.
>
> "Ceres yard has them," Ines says.
>
> "Ceres yard wants sixty for them."
>
> She's watching a message from her mum. It's eleven minutes old by the time it plays. "The lift's out again," her mum says. "The service charge went up in March, and now the lift people only come on Thursdays. Your brother's been carrying Mrs Duarte's shopping up."
>
> The nav software flags a change to the burn plan, then takes it back. Ines doesn't look up.

Read it before writing anything. It is the bar.

## How people talk

1. **People talk like people now.** Contractions, short answers, half-finished thoughts, slang a century on from ours but recognisable. They complain, joke, change the subject, and don't say everything.
2. **They answer the question asked,** plainly and a little offhand. Asked about home, someone says what it's like, who's still there, why they left, in that kind of order and that kind of length. A personal thing comes out sideways, later or not at all.
3. **They don't explain their world.** Nobody tells anyone what a service charge or a recruitment debt is. They mention it the way we mention rent. If the reader needs a fact, it comes out in what someone does or argues about.
4. **They only know what they would know.** A mother knows the lift is broken and when the lift people come. She doesn't know the repair schedule or the company's reasons. A hand knows their deductions to the credit and nothing about the finance house's books.
5. **No curated anecdotes.** No staircase with a hundred and twelve steps, no cat on the bread racks. People say ordinary things about ordinary lives, and the character is in what they choose to say.
6. **Feelings come in passing:** a shrug, a look to see if that's enough, a change of subject. A feeling is named only when the scene is about it, once, by the person who has it or about someone other than the player. The player's own feelings are never named.

## How the narration works

7. **State who owns, charges and pays, flatly.** "It isn't rated for heat. He'll log it as temporary." "Ceres yard wants sixty for them." No comment follows.
8. **Show extraction by setting facts side by side.** The service charge went up in March, and now the lift people only come on Thursdays. The reader connects them. Neither the narration nor the character does.
9. **No villains.** Hardship comes from the system working as it was built to, which is to extract wealth: prices, contracts, schedules, insurers' terms, lease conditions. Nobody is cruel on purpose. People inside the system mostly accept it, adjust and carry on.
10. **The technique doesn't show.** No narrator's asides about who bought what from whom, no aphorisms ("the charter payment is the ship's weather"), no scene titles, no closing lines that sum up the moment. Stop on a small action or a line of speech.
11. **Report what someone in the room could see or hear.** No similes that carry a feeling, no evaluative adverbs, no wry asides, no verdicts on the moment.

## Detail

12. **Every detail must be technically and socially plausible.** This has to be said because the old text failed it constantly: a boot used as a pressure seal, a mother quoting a repair list. Ask what a real engineer would do, what a real tenant would know, what a real pilot would say. If you don't know, look it up or leave the detail out.
13. **Specific, not vague.** Not "a street, a smell, the hour the bakery opened". A named thing that is actually there: the lift, Thursdays, Mrs Duarte's shopping, sixty at the Ceres yard. One true specific beats three general ones.
14. **From this world, not ours and not the Expanse's.** The detail comes from the bible: the tiers, the wall rota, the endorsement, the cheap relay slot, the trade notes. If it isn't in the bible and you need it, add it there first.

## Before and after

Before (the current game):

> You say nothing, and let the silence hold, and {n} goes on about smaller things: a street, a smell, a name, the hour the bakery opened. It's a long time before either of you looks at the clock.

After:

> "Lisbon?" Ines shrugs. "Crowded. It's an arcology, it's all crowded." She drinks her coffee. "My mum's still there. My brother. He fixes lifts, which in an arcology is basically being a doctor."

The before names a kind of detail instead of giving one, and narrates a mood. The after gives two facts about her family and a joke about her brother's job, and stops.

## Checking a passage

- Would a real person say this, at this length, to this person?
- Does anyone explain something the listener already knows?
- Does anyone know something they couldn't?
- Is every object and procedure something that would actually be done?
- Is there a vague detail where a specific one belongs?
- Is there a line that is the narrator's opinion, an aphorism, or a summing-up? Cut it.
- Is the economics in the open, stated flatly, and never commented on?
- Read it after the reference passage. Is it the same book?
