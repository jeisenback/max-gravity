# Work

## Who works, and who waits

Most people work for wages, on contracts that end. Behind every working person there are others who want the job: the unleased in every arcology (in Lisbon, the old city on the hills), the Belt squats. Agencies recruit there, and the wages offered anywhere are set by what those people would accept. A lease that comes with a job makes an arcology a company town: lose the job and you lose the home.

## How crews are hired

Most crews come through manning agencies, because insurers and registries require crew records held by an approved agency. An agency places a hand on a ship, charges a recruitment fee, and advances it as a debt repaid from wages. It holds the hand's record and ratings, and charges the shipowner a placement fee too.

Pay is a rate per day, set in the contract. Deductions come off first: the agency's repayment, berth, air and food, kit on hire, insurance. What is left is paid at a port, often late. When an owner fails mid-voyage, the crew can be left in port with no ship and no pay.

## Officers and ratings

A crew has two layers.

**Officers** hold licences: pilot, engineer, medic. They sign on the ship's own articles or a better agency contract, earn more, and often get a share of what the ship clears.

**Ratings** are everyone else: the bosun, deckhands, gunners on armed ships, cargo hands. They come through agencies, on lower pay with full deductions.

Most of a run's watches don't need an officer. The navigation software holds the ship on course under cruise thrust, and a rated watchkeeper on the bridge watches it and calls the pilot for anything that needs flying.

On a small ship officers and ratings eat at the same table and live on different terms. An officer can quit and take the licence elsewhere. A rating who quits still owes the agency.

## Remittances and households

Most hands send money home: to parents, children, siblings, the household's lease. A crew is a set of households in other places. A parent away for years is ordinary; children are raised by grandparents and paid for by remittances. Families talk by recorded message.

## Licences and records

Skilled work needs a licence, issued by a board for a fee, renewed every few years, and suspendable or revocable by the board that issued it. Other jurisdictions honour it or not. Cheap registries sell their own endorsements: legal on their ships, worthless elsewhere, and tying the holder to that registry's ships.

A hand's record follows them through the agencies: voyages, ratings, incidents, complaints. A bad mark can keep someone off the better ships for years.

## What people do about it

They organise where they can. Dockers and the crews of the big freighters have unions, which the owners and agencies fight; a strike at a depot is news across the Belt. Tenants' associations in the arcologies negotiate service charges and repairs. Crews file wage claims at ports that allow them. People evade: cash work, unregistered berths, false records. And they leave, which is what most of them do.

## Hester's charter

Captain Hester Vance runs her ship on a hire-purchase charter from a finance house. Each monthly payment includes a share of the ship's price. She is eleven years into a fifteen-year term. If she misses three payments, the house takes the ship back, with everything she has paid. She owns it only after the last payment.

She signs her officers on her own articles, at a day rate plus six percent each of what the ship clears after fuel, fees and the charter payment. Her ratings come through an agency, because her insurer requires it.

## Assumptions to confirm

- The officers' six percent is each, after fuel, fees and the charter payment.
- Hester raised the charter's deposit from twenty years of a hand's savings and a loan against her own future share, which she has since paid.
