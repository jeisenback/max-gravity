# Boneyard

Status: approved by the owner on 2026-11-04. Built on `economy.md`, `work.md`, `jurisdictions.md` and `technology.md`.

## The place

Three hundred dead ships lashed together into a town at Hygiea, their hulls welded into streets, their cargo bays turned into shops and homes. The salvagers who live here will sell you anything that came off a wreck, from a hull plate to a wedding ring, and never ask where your ship came from. At night the old running lights are switched on in patterns.

## Who owns it

Nobody owns the whole of it. Each ship in the town belongs to the family that towed it in, and the town is the agreement among them: who welds to whom, who shares air, who pays for the common scrubbers. The insurers leave it alone because it's where wrecks they've paid out on end up, and sometimes where they buy back parts.

## Hulls and bays

A salvage family owns a hull in the town, its own salvage tug and the right to work the wrecks it tows in. The hands who crew the tugs don't own anything. They're paid a share of each salvage, and rent a corner of a salvage family's cargo bay, and move from family to family as the work goes.

## Living there

The salvage families live in their own hulls, each with its own name, its old registry plate on the hatch. The hands' families live in the bays, curtained off, under someone else's name. The running lights at night are the families signalling to each other, a habit from when they were all still ships.

## Growing up here

**In a family hull.** A child of a salvage family, raised in the family's own dead ship, welded into the town, working the tug from the age they could fit through a crawlway. They know what every part of a ship is worth, broken. Who is missed: a parent who runs the family tug. Why they leave: the tug takes one child, and the town has more children than wrecks. What they hope for: a hull of their own, towed in by themselves, or a ship that isn't dead.

**In a cargo bay.** A child of a family of tug hands, raised behind a curtain in a corner of someone else's cargo bay, moving bays as the work moved. They know the town well and own none of it. Who is missed: a parent who crews someone else's tug. Why they leave: a share of a salvage is good money in a good year, and there aren't many good years. What they hope for: a family hull of their own, or a berth on a ship that pays a wage.
